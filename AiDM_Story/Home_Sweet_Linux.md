# Home Sweet Linux

## The Road from Windows and IDM to Building AiDM

---

# Chapter 1 — The Download Manager I Couldn't Leave Behind

Long before Linux became home, before I knew what a kernel really meant outside a textbook, before Git branches and browser extensions entered my daily vocabulary, there was Windows.

For most of my early life with computers, Windows was not simply an operating system. It was *the* operating system.

I started using computers around 2012, when I was about twelve years old. In the world around me, saying "computer" almost automatically meant Windows. Linux was not part of ordinary conversation. macOS belonged to a different universe. Windows was the desktop, the Start menu, the folders, the installers, the games, the errors, the fixes—and, eventually, the place where I learned to become curious about what was happening underneath all of it.

I moved through the familiar generations: Windows XP, then newer releases, eventually Windows 10 and Windows 11. For years I had no reason to imagine that one day I would leave that ecosystem completely.

And somewhere along that road, I met a small piece of software that became strangely difficult to imagine living without.

Internet Download Manager.

IDM.

Three letters that would stay with me much longer than I could have guessed.

## When Downloading Was the Only Sensible Option

The internet I grew up with was not the kind of internet where you casually opened a streaming service, selected 4K, and forgot that bandwidth existed.

Around 2016 and 2017, watching something online could be an exercise in patience. A movie might buffer. A cartoon might stop at exactly the wrong moment. A connection could slow down without warning. If I wanted to watch something properly, downloading it first often made far more sense than trying to stream it.

Movies, animated films, cartoons—if I wanted them, I wanted them *on the disk*.

That made a download manager feel less like a convenience and more like infrastructure.

IDM was fast, familiar, and wonderfully practical. It integrated itself into the browser and seemed to understand something I did not yet understand: where the media actually lived.

To me, that little download panel appearing over a video felt almost magical.

The browser could be showing a webpage full of buttons, players, scripts, advertisements and distractions, but IDM somehow seemed to look through all of that and say:

*There. That's the file you actually want.*

At twelve or sixteen, I did not care about request headers, manifests, cookies, session context, adaptive streaming, or browser network APIs. I only cared that I could press a button and the thing arrived on my computer.

That simplicity became normal.

And once something becomes normal, you only discover how valuable it was when it disappears.

## The One-Month Ritual

There was one problem.

IDM was commercial software.

Its trial lasted roughly a month, and I was a kid who was not exactly maintaining a software-subscription budget.

But at that stage I had another problem too: after using Windows for a while, I often felt that the system became slower and messier. A fresh installation felt clean again—quick boot, fewer leftovers, fewer mysterious background things, and that wonderful feeling of a computer returning to its original sharpness.

So an odd little cycle developed.

Windows would become cluttered.

IDM's trial would run out.

And eventually I would reinstall Windows.

A fresh operating system arrived.

And, conveniently, so did a fresh IDM trial.

Looking back, it was an absurdly large hammer for two completely different nails. I was effectively reinstalling an entire operating system while also resetting my relationship with a download manager.

But at the time, it worked.

There was something satisfying about that clean beginning: Windows was fast again, IDM was alive again, and everything felt new.

Eventually, of course, I discovered the other route that countless curious computer users eventually discover.

Cracks. Patches. Workarounds.

In my little corner of the world, that felt like forbidden technical knowledge. I remember the satisfaction of getting IDM to keep working beyond its normal trial period and feeling as though I had unlocked some secret level of computing.

It was not elegant. The modified versions could be stubborn. Errors appeared. Downloads sometimes behaved strangely. The software could work beautifully one day and irritate me the next.

Still, IDM remained.

I tolerated the cracks because the capability mattered more than the inconvenience.

That detail would become important years later.

I was already developing a habit without realizing it: when a tool gave me something I considered essential, I would endure quite a lot to keep that capability alive.

## Learning to Fight Windows

As I grew older, my relationship with Windows changed too.

During my Bachelor's degree in Computer Science, I became much more comfortable with the system. I was no longer only clicking icons and installing programs. I was learning where things lived, what services were doing, what happened at startup, what consumed memory, and why two apparently identical Windows installations could feel completely different.

Windows 10 became my favorite Microsoft operating system.

I liked it.

But I also started fighting it.

The more I understood computers, the more aware I became of everything running in the background. Services I did not need bothered me. Startup programs bothered me. Telemetry bothered me. Preinstalled applications bothered me. Processes using RAM while apparently doing nothing bothered me.

Gaming made the obsession worse.

When every frame, every gigabyte of memory, and every unnecessary background process feels like something stealing resources from the thing you actually want to run, Task Manager becomes less of a utility and more of a courtroom.

I began reading optimization guides.

Then debloating guides.

Then tutorials for disabling services.

Then explanations of startup tasks.

Then PowerShell commands.

A new Windows installation was no longer something I simply *used*. It became something I tuned.

I would remove things, disable things, change settings, inspect memory consumption and keep trimming until the machine felt like mine again.

And for a while, I enjoyed it.

There is a certain pleasure in taking a messy system and making it leaner. You learn something. You gain control. The computer responds better because of choices you made.

But there is a thin line between *customizing your system* and *constantly negotiating with it*.

I did not notice when I crossed that line.

Not yet.

## Windows Was Still Home

This is important: I did not spend those years dreaming about escaping Windows.

Quite the opposite.

Windows was familiar. I knew how to fix it. I knew where the settings were. I knew the shortcuts. I knew what software existed for it. When something broke, there were usually hundreds of tutorials explaining exactly how somebody else had broken the same thing.

And, of course, it had IDM.

I had heard of Linux. As a Computer Science student, how could I not?

But Linux lived mostly in the academic part of my brain. It was something associated with servers, programming, terminals, operating-system concepts and technically adventurous people.

It was not *home*.

Every now and then I would look at Linux distributions—especially Linux Mint—and wonder whether I could actually live there.

Then reality would answer.

Where was IDM?

Where were the familiar applications?

Why did so many instructions begin with opening a terminal?

What happened if I entered the wrong command?

And why would I leave an operating system I had spent years learning how to control just to become a beginner again?

So I stayed.

Linux remained a door I occasionally opened, looked through, and quietly closed.

Windows still had the stronger gravity.

## Then Came Windows 11

When Windows 11 arrived, I wanted to like it.

New operating systems are exciting when you enjoy computers. A redesign feels like a new machine without buying new hardware. There are new animations, new menus, new ideas to explore.

There was only one awkward detail.

My old Lenovo laptop was not officially invited.

It had 8 GB of RAM, a fourth-generation Core i5, and no TPM configuration that satisfied Microsoft's new requirements. Windows 11 looked across the room at my perfectly usable laptop and effectively said:

*Not you.*

Naturally, that made me want to install it more.

I found the workaround.

The installer complained about unsupported hardware and TPM requirements, but eventually Windows 11 booted on the machine anyway.

For a while, the victory felt wonderful.

I had the newest Windows running on hardware Microsoft did not want running it.

Another workaround.

Another little battle won.

At the time I saw that as proof that I could keep bending Windows into the system I wanted.

I did not yet understand what was changing.

The real problem was no longer going to be whether I *could* find another workaround.

The problem was going to be how many workarounds I was willing to live with.

And Windows was about to start asking that question much more often.

# Chapter 2 — When Windows Started Fighting Back

At first, Windows 11 was just another system to tame.

I had already spent years learning that Windows rarely had to remain exactly as Microsoft delivered it. If something annoyed me, there was usually a setting. If there was no setting, there was a registry entry. If there was no registry entry, somebody on the internet had probably found a PowerShell command.

That had been part of the fun.

Windows gave me a system.

I turned it into *my* system.

But Windows 11 slowly changed the nature of that relationship.

The little battles became more frequent.

And, more importantly, they started feeling less optional.

## The Operating System That Kept Coming Back

There is a particular kind of frustration that comes from removing something you never asked for, only to discover that it has returned later.

I knew that feeling well.

I would clean up the Start menu, remove applications I did not want, reduce background activity, change privacy settings, disable services, and try to keep the system lean.

Then another update would arrive.

Something would reappear.

Something would reset.

Something new would be promoted.

Edge wanted attention.

OneDrive wanted attention.

Microsoft accounts wanted attention.

The operating system increasingly seemed interested in telling me how Microsoft wanted Windows to be used.

And I increasingly wanted it to stop talking.

The funny thing is that none of these annoyances, taken individually, would have been enough to make me leave.

That is how these things work.

One unnecessary application is not a reason to abandon an operating system.

One background service is not a reason.

One notification is not a reason.

One forced restart is not a reason.

One setting that needs to be changed again is not a reason.

But irritation has a way of accumulating quietly.

At some point you stop asking, *Can I fix this?*

You start asking, *Why am I still fixing this?*

That was the question Windows 11 kept pushing me toward.

## Debloating Became Maintenance

By then, I had become good at cleaning Windows.

Too good, perhaps.

I knew which services I could inspect, which startup entries I could disable, which applications could be removed, and which tweaks were worth keeping after installation. I could search through Task Manager and spot processes that looked unnecessary. I knew how to chase RAM consumption and shave away background activity.

There was satisfaction in that.

But satisfaction slowly became routine.

Then routine became maintenance.

A fresh Windows installation was no longer followed by, *Great, now I can work.*

It was followed by an invisible checklist.

Remove this.

Disable that.

Change this setting.

Check those services.

Deal with Edge.

Deal with OneDrive.

Reduce startup applications.

Check telemetry settings.

Inspect memory again.

Make sure an update did not undo something.

Only after all of that did the machine begin to feel like mine.

That realization bothered me more than any single Windows feature ever had.

I was spending time making the operating system disappear so that I could finally use the computer.

## My Priorities Had Changed

There was another difference now.

I was no longer the same person who had once happily reinstalled Windows partly because an IDM trial had expired.

I had completed my Bachelor's degree in 2024.

By early 2025, I was doing my Master's.

The computer was becoming less of a playground and more of a workstation.

There were assignments, research tasks, code, machine-learning experiments, papers, datasets, notebooks, deadlines.

My time had changed value.

Hours spent tweaking Windows were hours not spent working on something that actually mattered to me.

That did not mean I suddenly stopped caring about performance.

If anything, I cared more.

But now I wanted performance to serve the work.

I did not want maintaining the operating system to *become* the work.

The distinction was becoming impossible to ignore.

## The Laptop That Should Have Fixed Everything

Eventually I moved to a newer machine: an HP EliteBook 840 G6.

Sixteen gigabytes of RAM.

An NVMe SSD.

Hardware comfortably newer than the old Lenovo.

And, importantly, hardware that did not need tricks to satisfy Windows 11's official requirements.

This should have been the clean reset.

No unsupported-hardware warning.

No TPM workaround.

No excuse to blame an aging laptop.

This time Windows 11 had the machine it wanted.

And I had enough hardware that an operating system should have had no reason to feel heavy.

Yet something still bothered me.

I would look at the system while it was sitting there doing almost nothing and see memory consumption that felt absurdly high to me—often several gigabytes before I had even begun serious work.

Open the browser.

Open a few tools.

Start working.

And the numbers climbed.

Now, to be fair, unused RAM is not automatically good RAM, and modern operating systems use memory for caching and background functionality. I understood that.

But this had become more than a technical argument about whether a particular number in Task Manager was objectively acceptable.

It was about trust.

I had bought better hardware.

I wanted those resources available for *my* workloads.

My models.

My browser tabs.

My code.

My experiments.

I did not want to feel that the operating system had arrived first and reserved a generous portion for itself before I had even sat down.

The EliteBook removed the final easy excuse.

The problem was not simply that my old laptop was too weak.

I was becoming dissatisfied with Windows itself.

## The Interruptions

Resource usage was only part of it.

There were the interruptions.

Updates that seemed to arrive on Windows' schedule rather than mine.

Restart prompts.

Occasional instability after updates.

Moments when something that had been fine yesterday behaved differently today.

A crash here.

A lag there.

The occasional blue screen.

And every time something went wrong after an update, the old troubleshooting instinct returned.

Search the error.

Read forum posts.

Check drivers.

Roll something back.

Run another command.

Restart.

Test again.

I could usually recover.

That was almost the problem.

Because being capable of fixing something can hide how much energy you are spending fixing it.

Windows had trained me well enough that I could keep surviving the relationship.

But survival was no longer the standard I wanted from my main workstation.

I wanted calm.

## Microsoft Was Moving in One Direction

At the same time, Microsoft's direction increasingly felt different from mine.

Windows was becoming more connected to Microsoft's services and accounts. Edge was integrated aggressively. OneDrive was difficult to ignore. More parts of the experience encouraged online accounts and cloud-connected workflows.

Then AI started becoming part of the operating-system conversation too.

Copilot appeared.

AI itself did not bother me.

Quite the opposite.

I was studying artificial intelligence. I was using AI tools. I was fascinated by what they could do.

What bothered me was the feeling that yet another layer was being pushed into an operating system that I was already trying to make quieter.

I did not want Windows to become more involved in my computing life.

I wanted the operating system to get out of the way.

That difference sounds small.

It was becoming enormous.

Microsoft seemed to be asking:

*How many more things can Windows provide?*

I was asking:

*How little can the operating system consume while letting me do my work?*

Those are two very different philosophies.

## Watching Other People Get Frustrated Too

As I became more interested in technology beyond my own machine, I started following tech news more closely.

And something unexpected happened.

For years, I had wondered whether my frustration with Windows was simply a personality problem.

Maybe I cared too much about RAM.

Maybe I was too aggressive about debloating.

Maybe normal people simply used the computer instead of opening Task Manager every ten minutes like a suspicious accountant.

That possibility was entirely reasonable.

But the more I followed discussions around Windows 11, the more I realized that dissatisfaction with Microsoft's direction was hardly unique to me.

Other users were complaining too.

Different people cared about different things—updates, account requirements, interface changes, cloud integration, regressions, removed control, unwanted features—but the underlying feeling was familiar.

The operating system increasingly wanted a say in how the computer should be used.

I did not enjoy seeing other people struggle with problems.

What I felt was something closer to validation.

Maybe I was not imagining the shift.

Maybe the relationship really had changed.

That thought would become much more satisfying later, when I could watch Windows controversies from somewhere else entirely.

But I was not somewhere else yet.

I was still sitting inside Windows.

## The Obvious Alternative I Kept Rejecting

There was, of course, an operating system that seemed philosophically much closer to what I wanted.

Linux.

It had been waiting in the background for years.

Open.

Customizable.

Lightweight when configured well.

Built around the idea that the machine belonged to the person using it.

And every time Windows irritated me enough, Linux Mint appeared in my mind again.

I had looked at it before.

More than once.

The desktop was friendly.

The layout felt familiar.

People often recommended it to Windows users.

It seemed like the Linux distribution most likely to meet me halfway.

Then the doubts returned.

IDM was not there.

Some familiar Windows applications were not there.

The terminal still looked like a place where one bad command could turn an operating system into smoke.

If something went wrong, nobody around me was using Linux.

There would be no friend sitting beside me saying, "Oh, I had that problem last week."

And I had a Master's degree to complete.

Was this really the right time to voluntarily replace the operating system I knew with one I did not?

Every practical instinct said no.

Windows annoyed me, but I could use it.

Windows fought me, but I knew how to fight back.

Linux offered freedom, but freedom came with responsibility.

So once again, I looked through the door.

And once again, I hesitated.

## I Was Tired of Fighting

By the end of 2025, something had changed.

Not in Windows.

In me.

I no longer wanted to win the operating-system battle.

That sounds like surrender, but it wasn't.

I had simply stopped caring about the prize.

What did I gain by successfully removing another unwanted component?

What did I gain by reducing another background process?

What did I gain by discovering another workaround after an update changed something?

A cleaner Windows installation?

And then what?

Wait until I had to do it again?

I had already proven to myself that I could optimize Windows.

I had already proven that I could work around Microsoft's requirements.

I had already proven that I could keep the system under control if I spent enough time doing it.

The question was no longer whether I could.

The question was why I should.

For the first time, leaving Windows no longer felt like losing something familiar.

It started to feel like putting down something heavy.

There was still one chain I could not ignore.

IDM.

The software that had been part of my computer life for years.

The tool whose absence had helped send me back from Linux before.

The thing I had once considered almost essential.

But even that relationship had changed.

My internet was better than it had been in 2016.

I was no longer a kid collecting every movie and cartoon I wanted to watch.

My Master's work mattered more than maintaining the perfect downloading setup.

Maybe I did not need IDM badly enough anymore to let it choose my operating system.

That was a dangerous thought.

Because once I allowed myself to think it seriously, the biggest reason for staying with Windows began to weaken.

And the door I had been opening and closing for years suddenly did not look quite so frightening.

On the other side was Linux Mint.

This time, I was not merely curious.

I was tired enough to walk through.
